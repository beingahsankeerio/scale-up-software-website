AI Receptionists and UK GDPR: What Businesses Need to Know

6 November 2026 · Scale Up Softwares

It is a Thursday afternoon and a new patient rings your clinic. They give their name, their phone number, and the reason they are calling — a toothache that has kept them awake for two nights. Your receptionist is with another patient, so the call is answered by your AI receptionist instead. It takes the details, books an appointment, and emails your team a summary — and your business now holds that caller's personal information.

What happens to that information next is where UK GDPR comes in. This post explains, in plain English, what UK GDPR means when an AI voice agent answers your calls: what data is collected, who is responsible for it, what a data processing agreement should cover, and the questions worth asking any provider before you switch one on. This is general information, not legal advice.

What UK GDPR actually asks of your business

UK GDPR applies whenever your business collects or uses personal data — a name, a phone number, anything that identifies a living person. An AI receptionist does exactly that on every call it answers, so the rules apply from the first call.

The law gives your business and your provider different roles. Your business is the data controller: you decide why the data is collected and what happens to it. Your provider is the data processor: they handle the data on your instructions, to run your agent, and should not use it for anything else. Knowing which role you play matters, because the controller is responsible for making sure the processing is lawful.

That starts with having a lawful basis. For most reception calls the basis is straightforward: the caller rang you and asked for help, and you need their details to book them in or call them back. Collect only what the call needs — a name, a number and a short reason for calling are usually enough. An agent that asks for bank details, passwords or a full medical history is collecting data it has no business holding.

Finally, your privacy notice should say what happens on your calls. If an AI assistant answers the phone and calls may be recorded or summarised, say so in plain language on your website — and, for clinics, in your patient information.

What data does an AI receptionist collect?

On a typical call, an AI receptionist collects the caller's name and number, the reason for the call, and the details of any appointment booked. After the call, your team receives a written summary so they can act on it. If calls are recorded, the recording and the transcript are personal data too, and need the same care as anything else you hold.

What it should not collect is anything the call does not need. Our agents are set up around your business — your opening hours, your appointment types, your booking rules — and ask only for the details that let them book the appointment or pass the message on. Anything clinical, personal or complicated goes to your team, not into a call summary.

The data processing agreement: what to look for

When another company handles personal data on your behalf, UK GDPR requires a written agreement between you and them: a data processing agreement, or DPA. It is the document that pins down who does what with your callers' data.

A good DPA should cover, at minimum, what data is processed and why; where it is stored; how long it is kept; who can access it; whether any sub-processors are involved; what security measures are in place; how breaches are reported to you; and what happens to the data when the contract ends — deleted, or returned to you. Read it before you sign.

We agree a data processing agreement with each client, explain where data is stored and how long it is kept, and use data only to run their agent. If a provider will not agree a DPA, or cannot answer where your data lives, do not switch them on.

Call recordings and telling your callers

Callers should know two things when they ring you: that they are speaking to an AI assistant, and that the call may be recorded or summarised. Hiding either fact breaks trust, and trust is what a receptionist — human or AI — runs on.

Our agents introduce themselves honestly as a virtual assistant, and callers can ask for a person or a call-back at any point. That introduction sets expectations: the caller knows they are talking to an assistant that books appointments and takes messages, not to a member of your team.

On recordings, the sensible rule is to keep them only as long as you need them — while a booking is being checked, say, or a complaint looked into. Keeping every recording forever, with no reason and no review date, is the kind of habit that causes problems. Agree a retention period with your provider, and make sure recordings are actually deleted when it passes.

The same thinking applies to a website chatbot or a WhatsApp booking agent: a visitor who chats with your website or messages you on WhatsApp is also sharing personal data, and your privacy notice should cover those channels too. If you are not sure it does, fix that before you add a new channel.

Health information needs extra care

Patient and health information is special category data under UK GDPR, which means it gets stronger protection than an ordinary name and phone number. If your AI receptionist answers calls for a dental practice, a clinic or an aesthetics business, this section matters most.

The practical rule is to take the minimum. An agent needs the general reason for a call — a check-up, a consultation, a toothache — so it can book the right appointment. It does not need a full medical history, a list of medicines, or the details of a condition. Those belong in a conversation with a clinician, not in a call summary.

If a caller starts giving clinical detail anyway, the agent should be set up to recognise that the call needs a human and pass it to your team straight away. The same goes for any sensitive call: a distressed caller, a complaint, or anything the agent cannot handle safely should reach a person quickly. For clinics, our guide to chatbots for clinics and practices covers the same questions for the website channel.

What an AI receptionist doesn't do

An AI receptionist does not take over your data protection responsibilities. You remain the data controller, and the decisions — what data is collected, how long it is kept, who sees it — stay with you.

It also does not give legal advice. This post is general information, not legal advice, and if your situation is unusual — you hold particularly sensitive data, or you have had a breach — speak to a data protection adviser or a solicitor before you decide anything.

And it does not decide on its own what to keep. Retention periods, deletion, and who gets access are set by you, in the DPA and in your own policies. If a provider cannot delete data on request, that is a problem with the provider, not with the idea of an AI receptionist.

Common questions

Is an AI receptionist GDPR compliant?
No provider can honestly promise blanket compliance for your business, because compliance depends on how the data is handled. What matters is the setup: a signed data processing agreement, data stored in a known location, a clear retention period, and an agent that collects only what the call needs.

Do we need a data processing agreement?
Yes. When another company handles personal data on your behalf, UK GDPR requires a written agreement. It should say what data is processed, why, where it is stored, how long it is kept, and what happens to it when the contract ends.

Where is our callers' data stored?
That depends on your provider, which is why it is worth asking before you sign up. We explain where data is stored and how long it is kept, and we use data only to run your agent.

Can a caller ask for their data to be deleted?
Yes. People have the right to ask for their personal data to be deleted in certain circumstances. Your provider should be able to delete a caller's details promptly when you pass the request on, and your privacy notice should explain how callers can ask.

Do we need to tell callers they are speaking to an AI?
Yes. Callers should know they are speaking to an AI assistant and that the call may be recorded or summarised. Our agents introduce themselves honestly as a virtual assistant, and callers can ask for a person or a call-back.

AI Receptionists for Vet Practices: Calls, Bookings, Emergencies

9 November 2026 · Scale Up Softwares

It is 9:05 on a Saturday morning. A dog owner is standing in her kitchen, her Labrador lying flat on the floor, refusing food and water. She rings your practice. The phone rings — and rings. Your receptionist is checking in a cat for a dental, the vet is mid-consult, and nobody can get to the phone.

By the time someone rings back, the owner has already rung the next practice on the list. That is the everyday reality behind the phrase AI receptionist for vet practices: in a vet practice, a missed call is rarely just a missed booking. It can be a worried owner, a pet in pain, or a regular client quietly moving their animals’ care somewhere else.

This post looks at what an AI receptionist does on a vet practice’s calls, how urgent calls reach a person fast, what it cannot do, and what it costs.

Why vet practice phones are different

Every appointment business misses calls. But a vet practice’s phone carries a wider mix of calls than most, and they arrive at the worst moments.

Worried owners. Many callers are anxious, and some are frightened. A dog that has eaten something it should not have, a cat that has stopped eating, a rabbit that is suddenly quiet — the caller wants reassurance and a plan, not a voicemail message.

Emergencies mixed in with the routine. A vaccination booking, a repeat prescription and a genuine emergency can all arrive on the same line, one after the other. Somebody has to sort them quickly, and that somebody is usually a receptionist who is already doing three other jobs.

Results, prescriptions and reminders. A large share of practice calls is not new bookings at all: blood results, repeat prescriptions, flea and worming reminders, post-operative check-ins. Each one is quick, but together they fill the phone lines and keep clients waiting.

The phone rings when the consult room is full. Calls cluster at opening time, at lunchtime and in the early evening, when owners are free to ring. Evenings and weekends are the hardest: the practice is closed, but that is when owners notice something wrong with their pet. Our guide to out-of-hours answering describes the same pattern in another kind of practice — and how many of those calls never get returned.

Voicemail does not fix it. Many callers who reach voicemail simply hang up and ring the next practice, rather than leave a message and wait for a call-back they are not sure will come.

What an AI receptionist does on a vet practice call

An AI voice agent answers the calls your team cannot get to — during busy spells, at lunch, in the evening and at weekends. It introduces itself honestly as a virtual assistant, and it works from the information you give it: your opening hours, your appointment types, your booking rules and your triage instructions. Our guide to what an AI voice agent is explains how it works in plain English.

On a vet practice’s calls, that usually means:

Booking routine appointments. Vaccinations, boosters, nail clips, six-month checks and health plans go straight into real available slots in your diary, and the caller gets the appointment confirmed on the call.

Taking messages properly. When a caller wants a call-back — about results, a prescription or a question for the vet — the agent takes the owner’s name, the pet’s name and the number to ring back, and sends you a summary of every call by email.

Answering the questions you hear every day. Opening hours, directions, parking, what to bring to a first appointment, how repeat prescriptions work. The answers come from your own information, so they stay accurate.

Reminders that cut no-shows. Appointment reminders by text reduce empty slots. Our guide to reducing appointment no-shows looks at the same problem from the other side — clients who book and then do not turn up.

For practices that take bookings by message as well, a WhatsApp booking agent can do the same job in chat, and a website chatbot can answer questions from owners who find you online at midnight and point them towards booking.

Emergencies: getting the urgent calls to a person, fast

This is the part of a vet practice’s phone that matters most. An AI receptionist cannot diagnose an animal. It cannot tell a worried owner that their pet will be fine, and it must never try.

What it can do is follow your triage rules. Before it goes live, you agree the words and situations that mean “urgent”: collapse, seizure, heavy bleeding, difficulty breathing, a pet that has been hit by a car, a suspected poisoning. When a caller describes one of those, the agent does not book them in for next Tuesday. It transfers the call to your team straight away — or, outside hours, to your on-call vet or your out-of-hours provider, exactly as you instruct it to.

That transfer is included in the Growth plan and above, as urgent-call transfer. It is the feature to check first when comparing options, because for a vet practice it is not a nice extra. It is the whole point.

Two things matter when you set this up. First, write the triage rules with your clinical team, not guessed: the agent only knows what you tell it. Second, the escalation path must actually be staffed — an urgent transfer that rings an empty room is worse than no transfer at all.

Used this way, the agent makes sure routine calls never drown out the urgent ones. The receptionist not spending ten minutes booking a booster is free to pick up the call that really needs a person.

What it doesn’t do

An AI voice agent is not a member of your team, and it should not be sold as one. It cannot give clinical advice, diagnose a pet, decide whether a case is an emergency, or change a prescription. It cannot calm a distressed owner the way a person can. It works from the information you give it, and anything outside that goes to a person.

It also cannot replace your receptionist. A vet practice needs people at the front desk — for the client in reception with a carrier, for the difficult conversation, for the judgement only experience gives. The agent covers the calls your team cannot reach.

On data, the same care applies as in any practice. Common questions

Can an AI receptionist tell if a call is an emergency?
It cannot diagnose an animal, and it should never try. It follows the triage rules you set: words and situations such as collapse, seizure, heavy bleeding or difficulty breathing trigger an urgent transfer to your team straight away, while routine calls are booked or messaged.

Will callers know they are talking to an AI?
Yes. The agent introduces itself honestly as a virtual assistant at the start of the call. Callers can ask for a person or a call-back at any point, and anything urgent or complicated is passed to your team.

Can it book appointments into our diary?
Yes. It offers real available slots from your diary and confirms the appointment on the call, and you receive a summary of every call by email. It works best when the diary is kept up to date.

What happens to calls outside opening hours?
The agent answers around the clock. It books routine appointments, takes messages for the morning, and passes genuinely urgent calls to your on-call vet or signposts the caller to your out-of-hours provider, exactly as you instruct it to.
